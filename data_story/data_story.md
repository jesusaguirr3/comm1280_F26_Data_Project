# The month Philadelphia rushed to build

**Philadelphia issued 642 permits for new residential construction in December 2021, more than six times its usual monthly count. The city has yet to recover to its former pace.**

*By Jesus Aguirre and Lisandro Collazo*

For nearly twenty years, Philadelphia gave developers a deal that was hard to pass up: build a home and pay no property tax on the new building for ten years. Property owners still paid taxes on the land, but the city exempted the value of the construction. Critics argued that the program favored developers at the expense of other taxpayers. After years of debate, City Council scaled back the abatement.

Developers had until December 31, 2021, to qualify for the full tax break. After that, the exemption would gradually decline over ten years. The city also introduced a 1% tax on residential construction costs. With both changes approaching, developers took the old saying about the early bird to heart and rushed to secure their permits.

## The December rush

We analyzed Philadelphia's building permit records and counted the permits for new residential construction each month.

| Period | Permits |
|---|---:|
| January–October 2021 (monthly average) | 98 |
| November 2021 | 218 |
| December 2021 | 642 |
| January 2022 | 36 |

As the deadline approached, the floodgates opened. Philadelphia issued more than six times its usual number of permits in December 2021, and then the activity stopped as abruptly as it had started. January brought 36 permits, barely a third of a typical month before the rush. Although developers sometimes hurry to wrap up paperwork before the new year, the previous December saw only 201 permits. The usual year-end rush could hardly explain what happened in 2021.

## Did developers build more or simply build sooner?

Developers may have moved projects they already planned for 2022 to the front of the line to take advantage of the tax break. If that were the case, the surge in late 2021 should roughly offset the decline in permits over the following years.

| Measure | Permits |
|---|---:|
| Annual pace before the deadline | 1,177 |
| Extra permits issued during the rush | 664 |
| Annual average, 2022–2024 | 628 |
| Cumulative shortfall, 2022–2024 | 1,649 |

Between 2022 and 2024, Philadelphia issued approximately 1,649 fewer permits than its previous pace would suggest. The November and December rush accounts for only about 40% of that shortfall. Even after accounting for projects that developers may have moved ahead of schedule, nearly a thousand permits remain unaccounted for relative to the city's former pace.

## What else could explain the decline?

The tax change did not happen in a vacuum. Interest rates rose sharply in 2022, making it more expensive for developers to finance construction and for buyers to purchase homes. Higher borrowing costs may explain part of the decline, though we have not yet separated their effect from that of the tax changes.

A permit also does not guarantee that a developer will put a shovel in the ground. Some developers may have secured permits to lock in their tax benefits without any immediate plans to begin construction.

We originally planned to use commercial construction as a comparison group. However, a separate ordinance reduced the commercial tax abatement on the same date, and commercial permits also spiked by a factor of 5.6. We need to find a comparison group that neither ordinance affected.

## A wrinkle in the records

Our initial analysis suggested that new construction permits skyrocketed from 326 in 2016 to 10,281 in 2019. Yet the total number of permits across all categories remained between approximately 44,000 and 64,000 annually.

The explanation had more to do with paperwork than construction. Philadelphia had switched permit records systems, changing how it classified new construction. The older records end in March 2020. Comparing the two systems directly would have painted a misleading picture of the city's construction history, so we limited our analysis to records from April 2020 onward.

## What we found

Philadelphia's tax deadline brought a wave of permit applications, followed by three years of considerably lower activity. Between 2022 and 2024, the city issued roughly half as many residential construction permits annually as it did before the policy change.

The December rush appears to have borrowed from the future, but the numbers suggest that something else also slowed construction. Our next step is to determine how much of the decline we can attribute to the reduced tax abatement and how much to rising interest rates.

*Source: City of Philadelphia building permit records, downloaded November 24, 2026. The complete analysis and reproducible queries are available in the repository's `data_analysis` folder.*
